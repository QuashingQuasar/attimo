Monocultivar, single origin, single estate. You see these words more and more on the labels of better olive oils. They belong to an emerging category of high-quality olive oil, specialty olive oil, and they describe three things: which olive, which place and which farm.

## TL;DR

- **Monocultivar** (also monovarietal, single-variety) means every olive in the bottle was one variety: all Coratina, all Picual, all Koroneiki.
- Each olive variety has its own taste and its own range of polyphenols, the compounds behind olive oil's bitterness, pepper and health value.
- Blending isn't a problem in itself. A blend made for a balanced flavour names its varieties; a supermarket blend is made for volume and price, and names nothing.
- **Single origin** means the olives came from one named place. How big that place is depends on the producer: a country, a region, a valley.
- EU rules only allow a region on the front label for certified PDO or PGI oils, so a bottle that says "Italy" can still hold oil from one region.
- **Single estate** means one farm grew the olives. Often the same farm milled and bottled them too.
- All three are characteristics of [specialty olive oil](/blog/specialty-olive-oil), together with an early harvest, a printed harvest date and a published lab analysis.

## Monocultivar: oil pressed from one single variety

On a supermarket shelf, olive oils differ in two ways: the country on the label, Italy, Spain or Greece, and the words "extra virgin". For most people, that is where the differences end.

There is more in the bottle than the label shows. Olives come in over a thousand varieties, and each one presses into a different oil. Wine drinkers know the idea: Chardonnay and Pinot Noir are grape varieties, and a wine made from one tastes of that grape.

Monocultivar means that the oil is pressed from one single variety only. A few of the best known:

- **Coratina** (Puglia, Italy): bitter, green and peppery.
- **Arbequina** (Catalonia, Spain): soft and almondy, with little bitterness.
- **Picual** (Andalusia, Spain): tomato leaf and cut grass, with a peppery finish.
- **Koroneiki** (Peloponnese and Crete, Greece): a small olive that gives an intense, fruity oil, bitter and peppery.
- **Chetoui** (northern Tunisia): herbal, with strong bitterness and pepper.

### Each variety carries its own range of polyphenols

Varieties differ in their chemistry as well as their taste. The clearest difference is in polyphenols.

Polyphenols are compounds the olive makes to protect itself against sun, insects and infection while its seed is still forming. In the oil, they are what you taste as bitterness and as the peppery sting at the back of the throat. They are also behind most of the research on olive oil and health.

How many an olive makes is set in large part by its variety. Coratina olives are rich in oleuropein, the bitter compound that makes a raw olive inedible, and pressing turns it into the polyphenols the oil carries. Arbequina olives make far less of it. Pressed at the same ripeness, a Coratina gives an oil with several times the polyphenols of an Arbequina.

Our own Coratina and Nocellara, from the same harvest and tested at the same laboratory, measured 847 and 400 milligrams of polyphenols per kilogram of oil.

### Harvest timing moves an oil within that range

The variety sets the range. When the olives are picked decides where in it the oil lands.

Polyphenols peak while the olive is still green and decline as it ripens. Oil yield runs the other way: a ripe olive gives up more oil than a green one, sometimes twice as much.

So a Picual picked green in October gives less oil, more bitter and richer in polyphenols, than the same trees picked ripe in December. One variety can taste mild in one bottle and fierce in another.

### Why a single variety signals quality

A label that names one variety tells you what the oil will taste like before you open it. It also ties the producer to that olive.

A single variety can't be stretched with filler. Mixing in cheaper oil would change the taste and break the claim on the label.

In practice, a monocultivar oil also tends to come from one area and one harvest, because a variety is usually grown together in one region.

### Blending can make good oil too

Blending itself isn't a problem. In wine it's a craft, different grapes balancing each other, and olive oil can be blended the same way: a bitter Coratina rounded with a fruity Peranzana, for example. A single variety is no condition for specialty olive oil. A blend composed for a balanced flavour belongs to it too, and it names its varieties.

But big producers mostly blend for cost. A supermarket brand sells the same oil all year, at the same taste and price, in millions of litres. To keep that up, bottlers buy bulk oil from several countries and harvests, add cheaper late-harvest or older oil to hit a price, and mix it to the brand's usual taste.

The tell is on the label. A producer blending for flavour names the varieties. A producer blending for cost writes nothing at all: no variety, and no mention that it's a blend.

## Single origin: olives grown in one named place

Single origin means the olives came from one named place. The place can be a country, a region or a valley, depending on how precise the producer chooses to be. Single-origin coffee works the same way: the bag names the country, the region or the farm the beans came from.

For olive oil, the EU requires every extra virgin bottle to state its origin at country level: "Italy", "Spain", or "a blend of EU oils". A region such as Puglia or Jaén may only appear on the front label when the oil is certified as a protected origin, a PDO or PGI.

Certification takes time and money every year, and many smaller producers decide it isn't worth it. So a bottle that says "Italy" can still hold oil from one region, or from one grove.

The same word also appears on oil grown elsewhere. "Product of Italy" often means only that the oil was bottled or blended in Italy, from olives grown across Spain, Greece, Tunisia and elsewhere.

Producers who know their origin name it where they can: the region, town, mill and grove, on the back label, the website or the lab report.

A single-origin oil can still be a blend of several local varieties. And a monocultivar can come from anywhere: Coratina is also grown in Australia and California.

## Single estate: olives grown on one farm

A single-estate oil comes from one farm, and the producer names it.

Wine has the same idea in "château bottled" or "estate bottled": the estate that grew the grapes also made and bottled the wine. Olive oil estates work the same way. Some own a mill and press their olives within hours of picking. Others bring them to a nearby mill and collect their own oil.

Single estate is the most traceable of the three. Every bottle leads back to one set of trees and one producer.

It is also the smallest scale. An estate sells what its trees gave that year, and after a dry summer that can be half of a normal harvest. Most single-estate oil sells out within the season.

| Term | What it tells you | What it doesn't tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Monocultivar | Which olive variety | Where it grew, when it was picked |
| Single origin | Where the olives grew | Which varieties, how many farms |
| Single estate | Which farm grew the olives | When it was picked |

## Variety, origin and estate are marks of specialty olive oil

These three terms belong to an emerging category of high-quality olive oil: specialty olive oil. It stands to supermarket olive oil the way fine wine stands to table wine, or specialty coffee to a jar of instant.

Next to the variety and the origin, specialty olive oil is defined by when the olives were picked and how fresh the oil is.

Olive oil is fruit juice. It loses flavour and polyphenols from the day it is pressed, so specialty oil is sold fresh from the latest harvest, with the harvest date printed on the bottle. The best-before date tells you little: it is counted from bottling, which can happen long after pressing.

Specialty producers also pick early, as with the October Picual above. Early olives give less oil, with more polyphenols and more flavour.

The variety, the harvest timing and the freshness all decide how many polyphenols reach your bottle. Polyphenols carry both the flavour and the health value. The most transparent producers publish a lab analysis with the number.

On a label, that comes down to a named variety, a specific origin, a harvest date and, ideally, a lab-tested polyphenol count. Then taste it: fresh, high-quality oil is aromatic, a little bitter, with a peppery sting at the back of the throat.

## FAQ

### What does monocultivar olive oil mean?

The oil is pressed from one olive variety only. Monovarietal, single-variety and single-cultivar mean the same thing.

### Is monocultivar olive oil better than a blend?

A monocultivar is easier to judge: one variety has a recognisable taste and a typical polyphenol range. A blend made for flavour, with its varieties named, can be just as good. A blend that names nothing is usually made to hit a price.

### What is the difference between single origin and single estate?

Single origin names one place, which can be as large as a country. Single estate means one farm grew the olives.

### Is single-estate olive oil higher in polyphenols?

Not by definition. Polyphenols depend on the variety, how early the olives were picked, how fast they were pressed and how old the oil is.

### Which olive varieties have the most polyphenols?

Among widely sold varieties: Coratina from Italy, Koroneiki from Greece, Picual from Spain and Chetoui from Tunisia, especially when picked early. Arbequina sits at the low end.

### Why doesn't supermarket olive oil name the variety?

Most supermarket oil is a blend of varieties, origins and harvests that changes from batch to batch, so there is no single variety to name.
