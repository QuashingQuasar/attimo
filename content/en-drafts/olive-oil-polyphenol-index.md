# The Olive Oil Polyphenol Index: Every Brand That Publishes Its Polyphenol Numbers (2026/27)

Almost no olive oil producer tells you how many polyphenols are in the bottle. This page lists every brand we found that does: the published figures, the compounds behind them, the analysis method, and whether the lab report is open for anyone to check.

Full disclosure: we make olive oil ourselves, and our Coratina is in the index. The ranking method and inclusion rules are public and checkable, and every entry links to its source.

## TL;DR

- Polyphenols are the compounds behind olive oil's bitterness, pungency and most of its researched health effects. Typical supermarket oil carries 100–250 mg/kg; the top oils here carry ten times that.
- Fifteen producers worldwide publish polyphenol figures for their current oil, from Greece, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Morocco, Germany and the US. This page lists all of them.
- Totals from different lab methods are different currencies — the same oil tests at 874 on one method and 1,046 on another — so this index does not rank by total.
- The ranking uses the two documented compounds instead: oleocanthal + oleacein, the "D1 index" printed on University of Athens certificates and used by the high-phenolic world's own awards.
- Oils whose producers publish no compound values appear in a second, unranked list. A stated total alone cannot place an oil.
- The index is alive: producers who publish their analysis can submit it at the bottom of this page.

## The inclusion rules

Three rules decide who is in:

1. The figures are published by the producer, on the producer's own site. Retailer listings and award registries don't count.
2. The figures describe the current or previous harvest. Polyphenols decline as oil ages — a number from 2022 describes an oil that no longer exists.
3. Any accredited analysis method qualifies. The method is printed next to every number.

Everything below was verified on the producers' own pages in September 2026.

## How the ranking works

Ranking oils by their total polyphenol figure would be the natural thing to do, and it would be wrong. The totals come from different lab methods that count different compound sets in different units: in the published cases where the same oil was analysed by both families, the NMR total came out 20% and 48% above the chromatography total. Two brands below prove it on their own bottles.

So the ranked index uses the measure the high-phenolic field itself uses: **oleocanthal + oleacein**, the two most-researched compounds in olive oil, printed as the "D1 index" on every University of Athens certificate and used by the Aristoleo Awards to grade high-phenolic oils. Single named molecules hold up across labs in a way totals never can: in peer-reviewed testing on the same oils, oleocanthal measured by NMR and by calibrated HPLC agreed within ±10% (Molecules, 2021). The methods disagree about what a "total" is; they largely agree about a named molecule.

The consequence: an oil can only be ranked if its producer publishes the compound values. Producers who state a total and nothing else appear in the unranked list below the index, whatever the size of the total. A claim you can't verify can't outrank one you can.

## How the labs measure, and why the totals differ

"Total polyphenols" sounds like one measurement. It is at least three, depending on the lab.

**NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance)** is the method behind most certificates in this index, run by the World Olive Center for Health with the University of Athens. It identifies and sums a long list of individual phenolic compounds, including heavy derivative molecules that other methods partly miss. Because it counts more compounds, its totals read structurally higher.

**HPLC and the COI reference method** are the chromatography methods used by most accredited food labs, including the International Olive Council's official method for biophenols. They quantify a narrower, defined set of compounds, so totals read lower for the same oil.

**Folin-Ciocalteu** is an older colour-reaction method that estimates total phenolic activity rather than counting compounds, expressed in caffeic acid equivalents — again its own scale.

The gap has structural causes, not sloppy labs. NMR counts the full weight of the large aglycon molecules and captures compounds that degrade or convert inside a chromatography column — the University of Athens group has shown chromatography can create derivative artifacts on-column (J. Agric. Food Chem., 2014). The COI method, in the other direction, expresses its total in tyrosol equivalents: it counts the small core of each molecule, a units convention that reads low by construction. Even within a single COI certificate, the sum of the individually identified compounds can sit well below the stated total. And Folin correlates only loosely with compound-resolved totals — a 2026 study across a hundred top-rated oils found the two disagree on whether an oil clears the EU claim threshold in a third of cases (Antioxidants, 2026).

The gap is also measurable, because two brands above publish the same oil under both families: Olivea reads 874 by HPLC and 1,046 by NMR; PJ Kabos reads 995 and 1,473. Same oil, both numbers legitimate, 23% and 48% apart — and because the gap depends on each oil's phenolic profile, no fixed conversion factor exists. This index converts nobody's number. A projected figure a producer never published has no place in a list built on published figures.

The compounds behave differently. In peer-reviewed testing on the same oils, oleocanthal by NMR and by calibrated HPLC agreed within ±10% (Molecules, 2021): the disagreement between methods lives in the definition of "total", not in the measurement of a named molecule. That is the scientific basis for ranking on compounds. One asymmetry remains — COI certificates state compound lines in their conservative units too, which is why Olivea's oleocanthal reads 143 on the COI certificate and 244 on the NMR one. Chromatography rows in the ranking are understated by that asymmetry, never flattered.

## Why almost nobody publishes a number

Polyphenol content is the closest thing olive oil has to a measurable quality signal. It predicts bitterness and pungency, it decides whether an oil qualifies for the EU's only approved olive oil health claim, and it declines with age — a high number implies fresh, carefully handled oil.

Most producers still publish nothing, and the reason is structural: most oil would test poorly. Late-harvest, high-yield production — the economics behind nearly every supermarket bottle — gives oil low in polyphenols. Publishing a number would document that.

The fifteen brands above are a self-selected group. Publishing is itself the signal: it says the producer measured, and was willing to show the result.

No published number does not mean a poor oil. It means an unverifiable one. The producers above operate mills, commission analyses and see these figures every season; leaving them unpublished is a choice.

## FAQ

**Which olive oil has the highest polyphenol content?**

No single answer is possible across lab methods, which is why this index ranks by documented compounds instead: Laconiko ZOI leads with 1,196 mg/kg oleocanthal + oleacein (1,000 of it oleocanthal), followed by The Governor Limited (875 combined oleocanthal + oleacein) and ATTIMO Coratina (807). The largest published totals are Pamako's 2,000 and Sakellaropoulos Fyllikon's 1,825, both NMR figures. Higher totals exist in award registries — up to 3,400 mg/kg — but their producers publish nothing themselves.

**What counts as a high polyphenol content?**

The EU's approved health claim requires 5 mg of hydroxytyrosol and derivatives per 20 g of oil — roughly 250 mg/kg total polyphenols. Typical supermarket oil sits at 100–250 mg/kg. Everything in this index clears the claim threshold; the top entries carry around ten times a supermarket bottle.

**Why do two labs give the same oil different numbers?**

They count different things in different units. NMR sums the full weight of a long list of phenolic compounds; the COI method expresses its total in tyrosol equivalents, which reads low by construction; Folin-Ciocalteu estimates total phenolic activity chemically. Two brands in this index publish the same oil under two methods, 23% and 48% apart. Individual compounds are the exception: measured on the same oils, oleocanthal by NMR and by calibrated HPLC agrees within about 10%.

**What are oleocanthal and oleacein?**

The two most-researched individual polyphenols in olive oil. Oleocanthal produces the peppery catch at the back of the throat and has documented anti-inflammatory activity; oleacein is an antioxidant studied for cardiovascular protection. Average oils carry roughly 135 and 105 mg/kg respectively, per the UC Davis study referenced on University of Athens certificates.

**Does a higher number mean a better oil?**

It means a more polyphenol-dense oil, which predicts bitterness, pungency and the researched health effects. It says nothing about defects, freshness at the moment you buy, or whether you'll enjoy the taste — the top oils here are intense experiences. Quality still needs the basics: extra virgin grade, recent harvest, dark packaging.

**Why is my favourite brand missing?**

Either it publishes no polyphenol figure on its own site — the industry norm — or we missed it. The second has a fix: hello@attimo-oil.com.
